Winter 2026–27

California Is Preparing. Homeowners Should Too.

  • NOAA Climate Prediction Center · Sept. 10, 2026 El Niño is strengthening. An El Niño Advisory is in effect, with a greater than 90% chance of a very strong event during fall and winter 2026–27.
  • California Governor’s Office of Emergency Services · Sept. 21, 2026 Governor Newsom proclaimed a statewide state of emergency to prepare for El Niño’s anticipated impacts—directing state agencies to prepare roads and infrastructure, stage emergency supplies and help local partners reduce flood and landslide risks.

For homeowners: fall is when trees can be evaluated and needed work completed on dry ground, in calmer weather. Once storms arrive, tree crews across the region shift to emergency response.

What This Doesn’t Mean

El Niño does not guarantee that Nevada or Placer County will experience a particular storm or unusually wet winter. Seasonal forecasts describe broader climate patterns, not individual local storms.

The state’s own announcement says the same: El Niño “does not guarantee a wet winter or any particular storm.”

The Storm Doesn’t Create Every Hazard.

Sometimes It Reveals One.

Heavy rain, saturated soils, strong winds and snow can expose weaknesses in trees that may not be obvious during dry weather.

Saturated Soil

Prolonged precipitation can reduce soil stability and expose existing root-system problems.

Strong Winds

Wind can place significant loads on dead limbs, weak attachments and structurally compromised trees.

Heavy Rain & Snow

Additional weight on branches and canopies can contribute to failures in already weakened trees.

Trees Near Structures

Existing defects become much more consequential when a tree or large limb can strike a home, driveway, vehicle or other structure.

Before Winter Arrives

What Should Homeowners Look For?

  • Dead or hanging branches
  • Cracks or splits in trunks or major limbs
  • Cavities or visible decay
  • Mushrooms or fungal growth around the trunk or root area
  • Recent leaning or soil movement
  • Previous storm damage
  • Large compromised limbs over structures
  • Trees that have recently changed noticeably

Seeing one of these conditions does not necessarily mean a tree needs to be removed. When you’re unsure, professional evaluation can help determine the appropriate next step.
